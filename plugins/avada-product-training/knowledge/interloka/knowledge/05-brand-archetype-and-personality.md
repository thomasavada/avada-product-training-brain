# Section 4 — Brand Archetype & Personality

**Source range:** `IMG_7188`–`IMG_7193`.

## [SOURCE] Role of archetypes

The course traces archetype theory from:

- Carl Jung — collective unconscious/archetypal patterns;
- Joseph Campbell — cross-cultural heroic patterns;
- Margaret Mark & Carol S. Pearson — application to marketing/branding in *The Hero and the Outlaw*.

Archetypes help structure a recognizable role, desire, fear, strategy, strength, and weakness.

## [SOURCE] Examples

`IMG_7189` includes:

- **Duty-fulfiller / responsible type:** seriousness, durable value, loyalty, responsibility, fear of failing the duty.
- **Outlaw:** breaks ineffective rules, seeks freedom/revolution, uses disruption/shock.

`IMG_7191` includes **Creator**: imagination, creativity, innovation, vision.

`IMG_7192` uses VietJet as an **Inspirer** expressed consistently across ticketing, check-in, aircraft experience, and communication.

## [SOURCE] Brand personality groups

`IMG_7193` maps personality traits with reference to Aaker’s Brand Personality and Big Five models, including broad groups such as:

- sincerity;
- excitement;
- competence;
- sophistication;
- ruggedness.

Examples include Disney, Tesla, Red Bull, Volvo, Google, Tiffany, Rolex, Timberland, Amazon, Coca-Cola, Intel, Gucci, Jeep, and Marlboro.

## [SOURCE] Application requirement

The source demonstrates that archetype/personality must appear across touchpoints, not only in copy. It shapes behavior, service, visual expression, and experience.

## [SYNTHESIS] Quality test

- Which customer identity or aspiration does the archetype support?
- What desire, fear, strategy, and shadow does it imply?
- How does it change product/service behavior?
- Which touchpoints make it recognizable?
- Which behaviors would violate the archetype?

Archetype is a behavioral system, not a mood-board label.
